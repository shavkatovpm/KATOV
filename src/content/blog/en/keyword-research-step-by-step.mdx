Keyword research is the process of figuring out exactly what words customers type in when searching for your product or service. Done properly, it builds a site's copy around what the customer is actually searching for — not around how you'd describe it internally.

Short answer: **keyword research is a simple 4-step process using free tools**: gathering terms, checking their frequency, identifying search intent, and placing them on the page.

In this article, we'll cover:

- where to find keywords for free, and how;
- the difference between Google and Yandex Wordstat, and why you need both;
- whether to target a high-volume keyword or a more specific long-tail one;
- how to actually place the keywords you find on a page.

---

## Step 1: where to gather keywords

Four free sources are enough to get started.

**Google autocomplete.** Type your service name into Google's search bar (e.g. "website creation") — the suggestions that appear are phrases people are actually searching.

**The "People also ask" box.** Any search results page includes a box of related questions — a ready-made source of FAQ and blog topics.

**Google Trends.** Compares two terms for free and shows which is searched more often, plus seasonality (e.g. does interest in "New Year gift" spike in December).

**Google Search Console.** If you already have a site, this is the most accurate source — it shows the actual terms people use to find you. See our full guide on [how to use Google Search Console](/blog/how-to-use-google-search-console) for details.

### Yandex Wordstat — why it shouldn't be overlooked

Many users in Uzbekistan, especially in Tashkent, also search in Russian. Yandex Wordstat (wordstat.yandex.ru) is a free service that shows the monthly search frequency for any term — you just need a Yandex account. It surfaces data Google doesn't have, filling out a keyword list for a bilingual audience.

| Tool | What it's for | Cost |
|---|---|---:|
| Google autocomplete | Quick keyword ideas | Free |
| Google Trends | Seasonality and comparison | Free |
| Google Search Console | Real search terms driving your site | Free (if you have a site) |
| Yandex Wordstat | Russian-language search frequency | Free |

## Step 2: identifying search intent

Every keyword carries its own underlying goal. Two people searching "website cost" might be at completely different stages — one is still researching, the other is ready to order. It helps to split terms into three groups:

- **Information seeking**: "how much does a website cost," "what is SEO" — a blog article fits these best.
- **Comparison**: "cheap vs. expensive website," "template vs. custom build" — these need comparison-style content.
- **Ready to buy**: "order a website in Tashkent," "online store pricing" — these should point straight to a service page.

A page that doesn't match the intent behind a search (say, a long theoretical article for a purchase-intent query) ranks poorly in Google, because the visitor leaves right away.

## Step 3: high-volume keyword or long-tail?

The most common mistake for a new or small site is chasing only the highest-volume, most competitive terms (like "website creation"). Those pit you against large sites that have been operating for years.

**Long-tail** keywords (3-5 words, more specific) get searched less often, but competition is lower and conversion is higher — because the searcher already knows exactly what they want:

| Broad term (high competition) | Long-tail alternative (lower competition) |
|---|---|
| website creation | restaurant website creation in Tashkent |
| online store | opening an online store for a small business |
| SEO services | SEO services pricing in Uzbekistan |

For example, a [restaurant website](/services/restoran-sayti) and an [online store](/services/internet-dokon) shouldn't both chase the same "website creation" term — each should target its own niche-specific long-tail keywords, so each finds its audience faster.

## Step 4: how to actually place the keywords on a page

Once your list is ready, pick one primary keyword per page and place it naturally in these spots:

1. **Page title** — the primary keyword near the front.
2. **H1** — the single main heading on the page.
3. **Opening paragraph** — the keyword should appear naturally right at the start.
4. **Subheadings (H2s)** — related keyword variations.

One key rule: artificially repeating a word over and over (keyword stuffing) doesn't help — it actively hurts. Google can tell the difference between text written naturally for a person and text forced around a keyword. Readable, natural copy comes first; keyword placement comes second.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does keyword research matter?

It shows you the exact words customers use when searching for your product or service. Without it, a site gets written in language that makes sense to you but not the terms customers actually search — and Google never surfaces it.

### How do you do keyword research for free?

Google autocomplete, the "People also ask" box, Google Trends, and Yandex Wordstat are all free and need no sign-up. If you already have a site, the Performance report in Google Search Console is the most accurate source.

### Should you target a high-volume keyword or long-tail?

For a new or small site, long-tail keywords deliver results faster because competition is lower. Ranking for "website creation" is hard; ranking for "restaurant website creation in Tashkent" is far easier.

### Is Yandex Wordstat useful for businesses in Uzbekistan?

Yes. Many users in Uzbekistan, especially in Tashkent, also search in Russian. Wordstat shows the monthly frequency of those searches for free — data Google doesn't provide.

### How do you place keywords on a page?

The main keyword should appear naturally in the page title, the H1, and the opening paragraph. Repeating a word artificially doesn't help — text needs to read naturally for a person first.

### How often should you redo keyword research?

For a new page, a deep pass happens once at the start. After that, checking Google Search Console every 3-6 months for which terms drive visits is enough.

## Conclusion

Keyword research doesn't require complicated or paid tools — Google autocomplete, Google Trends, Search Console, and Yandex Wordstat are enough to get started. What matters is targeting long-tail keywords that match a customer's exact intent and face less competition, rather than chasing the most popular broad term.
